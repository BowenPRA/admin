## Writing style

- **Plain copy, said once.** Say the thing once, plainly, give the concrete detail, and stop. No neat line closing every paragraph, no set-up and pay-off, no point made twice, no em-dash asides in every sentence. Short sentences. Set 24 September 2026 ("These are horrible. This writing feels super like AI. write more clearly and naturally"). Why: it reads as a copywriter performing instead of a person telling a parent what happens in class, and Bowen spots it at once. He approved copy with some voice the day before, so the bar is no flourish for its own sake, not no voice.
- **The banned words.** Never unlock, empower, fostering or foster, journey, nurture, holistic, and on the website also elevate, vibrant, thrive, world-class, cutting-edge, seamless, robust, passionate, dynamic, transformative, "in today's world", "future-ready", "global leaders of tomorrow". No triads for their own sake ("Learn. Grow. Succeed."), no rhetorical questions as headings ("Why choose us?"), no emoji as bullets, no three identical icon cards in a row. Set 23 September 2026, with the longer list in the website's plan. Why: these are the marks of generic AI copy that could belong to anyone. <!-- allow -->
- **The website's voice.** Short, specific, warm, a little playful. "We" is the staff; talking to a parent it is "your child". Every page ends with one clear next step (book a visit, ask a question). Set 22 September 2026. Why: the site is for prospective parents, who need the facts and a way to act on them.
- **Facebook posts.** 50 to 90 words, warm, with the students as the ones who did the work: two or three highlights a reader can picture, grouped by what happened, never by the clock. No class-by-class lists, no times, never credit the photographer or name the performers. Captions in English only. Set 28 September 2026 (the third version was approved). Why: the first versions read like an itinerary, the next ones were too thin.
- **Photo captions on the website do not over-explain.** Say what the photo shows and stop. Set 23 September 2026 ("Do not over natrate or over share. remember this is facing to prospective parents"). Why: the reader is a prospective parent, not someone who was there.
- **Emails to staff are signed by the person who sends them.** Written in plain English, starting "Hey", not "Hi"; real bullet points are fine; Seth in copy. Set 24 September 2026 ("Sign them from Bowen (do not sound like AI written)"; "Use hey not hi"). Why: they go out under a person's name and must sound like that person.
- **The newsletter follows Seth's own format.** Its section headings carry emoji, as in Seth's Google Doc. Set 16 October 2026. Why: Bowen asked for the newsletter to match the Google Doc it replaced; the emoji rule above is about the website.

## Changing a rule

A rule changes when you say so, and only then. Tell Claude the new rule and why, in a session. Claude updates its memory note, this chapter, and the file where the rule is enforced (the triage's rules, the website's plan, an agent definition), then deploys The Current so the handbook shows it. A rule that the code enforces, such as a character limit or the word checker, is also a change to the code.

For example:

> **Ask Claude:** "From now on, Facebook captions may be in English and Vietnamese. Update your memory, the rules chapter of the handbook and the social-post skill, and deploy The Current."

> **Careful:** a rule written only in chat is forgotten by the next session. If Claude does something against a rule you set, ask it to check its memory note for that rule; if the note is missing or wrong, have it fixed there.
